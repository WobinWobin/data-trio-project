Wagon Master

1950

Directed by John Ford

Synopsis

John Ford's lusty successor to "Fort Apache" and "She Wore a Yellow Ribbon"

Two young horse traders guide a Mormon wagon train to the San Juan Valley and encounter rugged terrain, the cutthroat Clegg gang, hospitable Navajo, and moral challenges on the journey.

Review by Neil Bahadur 4

"We've passed through here once. Remember? You said that tower over there looked like...a Cathedral in Santa Fe."

A work so relaxed that Ford can halt narrative to linger, study & make dominant the simple movement of things. A poem of communities, circles, horses. But like Straub, there are still monsters. A killer whose name is based on a famous battle during the Civil War takes pride in the golden bed he sleeps in. But whenever there is an act of violence, it is shocking, more shocking than any Ford film, more than any other Western. Because this is a film which is not interested in the conventions the Western genre brings with it, yet it must acknowledge the reality of violence. And so Ben Johnson disposes with his gun at the end of the film. What's more important is that there is love, love between characters, and empathy for humanity. A true cinema pilgrimage - Ford's most beautiful film.

In place of narrative, good-naturedness wrestles with self-righteousness, a desire for peace with the inevitability of violence, "proper" behavior with the natural, vulgar beauty of humanity. Review by Ziglet_mir 11

The right way ain't always easy. The unknown hangs over the buttes and plateaus like anticipation on their breath, ethereal and majestic, as ghosts who witness the courageous. Heroism is sublime. Settlers bear their souls upon the monolithic cathedrals of the West, only sometimes mistaken as tombstones. Those ghosts hang, tacked to the air ready to haunt. Meanwhile communal song rings outward, rolling over vistas and rocky rivers, fading fog and frenzy. The path less traveled is gospel. Simple and understated, Ford's direction summons beauty with a tantalizing level of quaintness. Resiliency is reflected toward humanity back from nature, a mirror we all fail to hold at one time or another, as we are reminded the unknown can be frightening…

“Wagon Master” carries the glorious simplicity of unbridled hope — an optimism that bore thousands Westward, on nothing more than wooden wheels and a prayer.

“Wagon” can be viewed as Ford’s treatise on his particular vision of an American utopia.
