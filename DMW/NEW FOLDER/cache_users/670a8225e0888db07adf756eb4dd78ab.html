Directed by Harmony Korine

Synopsis

In the seedy domain of Miami’s criminal underbelly, a seasoned hitman embarks on the relentless pursuit of his next target. As shot entirely through thermal lens, he navigates a twisted world where violence and madness reign supreme. Tensions unravel, leading to a psychedelic journey that blurs the lines between predator and prey.

Review by Robert Franco 7

i see a lot of posts making fun of people who log a movie on letterboxd in a theater the second it’s over. i am perhaps the first person guilty of doing this at a strip club.

i am at the screening of this at LA strip club “crazy girls”. is it a great movie? hard to say. but it is the first example of truly pure cinema i’ve seen from the 2020s that invoked the work of phil solomon and stan brakhage. also, a little bit of terrence malick. 

there is a rare kind of artist that masters the medium so early on that they get bored of it and try to turn it into something different. this is not to say that they are the best at said medium but they certainly have the capability to be if they wished. harmony korine is one of those artists.

Review by Will Sloan

Pregamed this by eating a very special brownie, which felt great for a while, but definitely had me pretty drowsy by the end.

One thing that’s undeniable is that there are lots of colors to look at. The story, about a hit man who is also a family man, is a clear metaphor for Korine’s own concern about remaining cutting-edge at 50. Is this “the future of movies” or “something beyond movies,” as Harmony himself has said? No. It’s an example of what’s called an “experimental film,” which is something they’ve been making for a while. Check out Dog Star Man.

Whatever the case was in Venice, this was warmly received by the Midnight Madness audience. If there were any walkouts, I didn’t see them.

Review by davidehrlich

Harmony Korine has been openly bored with movies as we know them since the first time that he directed one. Real ’90s kids remember when he went on “Late Show with David Letterman” to promote “Gummo,” and insisted to the befuddled host that “things need to change. We can make films differently.” Korine may not have been wrong on either score back in 1997, but he’s a hell of a lot more right today. We live in a time when Hollywood offerings have become more stale than ever, and traditional cinema is beset on all sides by new technologies, novel coronaviruses, and — in Korine’s case — even some of the same artists who’ve helped to push the medium forward…
