Amistad

1997

Directed by Steven Spielberg

Synopsis

Freedom is not given. It is our right at birth. But there are moments when it must be taken.

In 1839, the slave ship Amistad set sail from Cuba to America. During the long trip, Cinque leads the slaves in an unprecedented uprising. They are then held prisoner in Connecticut, and their release becomes the subject of heated debate. Freed slave Theodore Joadson wants Cinque and the others exonerated and recruits property lawyer Roger Baldwin to help his case. Eventually, John Quincy Adams also becomes an ally.

Review by SilentDawn 4

Utterly underrated and terrifically done; Amistad is an inspirational mix of Spielbergian goodness and historical significance. Yes, It's sappy. Yes, It's sentimental. Yes, It drags at points.

However, It's also a beautiful tale that works wonders on the emotions, mainly because of John Williams and his knack for swelling the score at the perfect moment. The cast is excellent, the cinematography is stunning, and It has an opening scene that grips the audience in right away.

Overall, It's a smaller Spielberg work, but that doesn't mean it isn't great.

Review by matt lynch 1

While this offers the people whose lives are at stake much more of a presence (and a literal voice) than LINCOLN, it's more interesting now as a bookend with that film, especially since both are largely concerned with political language and procedure. But I'm not sure it fully transcends being just well-meaning. There's a curious moment, when Cinque and his people are first released by a lower court, and while everyone is celebrating, one of the Mende, still in chains, clutches a Bible. It's a telling moment of both historical transition and of hypocrisy, that supposedly God-given freedom is almost always subject to some other system or power structure. This movie continues to grow on me.

Review by Michael James

It is one skillfully crafted and disturbing historical drama, one that portrays an important tale of slave revolt and its legal consequences. Though there are some reservations on its historical accuracies, it does handle the sensitive subject matter sensibly enough. You get some intensely gripping and moving moments, however the cliched formulaic route does make it feel repetitive and slightly longer. It possesses an ensemble cast, however the ones who make the most impact are Djimon Hounsou and Matthew McConaughey. Anthony Hopkins scores with his big finale speech. Despite of the shortcomings, it definitely is worth the watch.
