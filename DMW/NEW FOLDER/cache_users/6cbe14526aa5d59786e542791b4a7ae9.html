Schlock

1973

Directed by John Landis

Synopsis

The first musical monster movie in years.

A quiet suburb in Southern California is terrorized by a mysterious murderous monster living in a cave. As the bodies pile up -- with incriminating banana peels always near by the crime scene -- a group of teens stumble on the guilty party: a 20-million-year-old Schlockthropus, an ape-like creature with a sense of the absurd.

Review by Marcel Dykiert

While it is undeniably true that John Landis has directed many magnificent films, allowing one to highlight the pseudo-documentary camerawork as well as the deliberate disruption caused by the Schlock trope within an everyday environment that would eventually become his professional trademark, the artistic merit of this particular piece remains debatable.

Even if we recognize the various references to "King Kong" or "Bride of Frankenstein" and take note of the very first appearance of his recurring "See you next Wednesday" gag, these elements merely serve as historical footnotes rather than saving graces for the production itself.

Despite John Landis having crafted a multitude of great cinematic experiences, "Schlock" simply does not rank among them regardless of how much affection one might hold for his broader body of work.

Review by Kai Perrignon 3

The central conceit is a pair of quotations: what if we made a “gorilla suit movie” in 1971? John Landis thinks that a gorilla suit is intrinsically entertaining, and I agree, but his ontological treatment of the ape suggests that he his appreciation for gorilla suit films does not extend beyond the purely aesthetic pleasure from the image of an obviously fake primate. In Schlock, the gorilla only haphazardly inspires fear, or awe, or just acknowledgement; for long stretches, he might as well be a hairy Tramp-like human being. The ape pantomimes animal instinct only when convenient for a joke, like when Landis stages an extended 2001: A Space Odyssey parody in which the gorilla steals a pile of bananas…

At this point Landis is most notable for the many terrible instincts he has had throughout his life, whether that be murdering three people, helping conceive Max Landis, or even Blue Brothers 2000, but his decision to make a gorilla suit comedy that inexplicably believes that the audience needs to be prompted to understand the absurd dissonance innate to the prowling gorilla subset via a contrast between no-laugh sequiturs and a barely-there animalistic instinct is right up there with the most oblivious of them all.
