Review by PTAbro 24

Ugh. Tatsuya Nakadai's cold, soulless eyes are terrifying.

At odds with my preconceived notion of cinematic samurai, Nakadai's Ryunosuke is established as amoral, even for a virtual mercenary. His progression into madness, coupled with his swordsmanship, make the film feel ahead of its time by following the 'bad guy' instead of having a tragic turn bring the protagonist to his lowest point within the film itself. Director Kihachi Okamoto further freshens the pic by sharing the narrative focus with several disparate groups that are either in Ryunosuke's crosshairs or are trying to put this mad dog down, so as not to overexpose the star's mystique and brooding dread. Finally, if Okamoto's stunning compositions (so dense!) or the prospect of seeing Toshiro Mifune decimate a group of rōnin in the driving snow isn't enough to turn you on to The Sword of Doom, I don't know what will.

Review by Jake Cole

The concepts of hollow honor and meaningless bushido code are nothing new in jidaigeki, but I struggle to recall even a present-day samurai film as utterly bleak as this. From the moment Ryunosuke materializes out of thin air to an elderly man's prayer for death to the freeze frame of him locked in a seemingly endless battle with every swordsman in the region, Nakadai exudes a completely bloodless, empty devotion to killing for its own sake. There's no tragedy to this existentially bound hitman, just the horror of see a raw nerve mastery of violence unbound from any manner of restraint or conduct.
