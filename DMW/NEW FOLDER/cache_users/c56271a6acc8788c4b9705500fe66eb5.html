Munich

2005

Directed by Steven Spielberg

Synopsis

The world was watching in 1972 as 11 Israeli athletes were murdered at the Munich Olympics. This is the story of what happened next.

During the 1972 Olympic Games in Munich, eleven Israeli athletes are taken hostage and murdered by a Palestinian terrorist group known as Black September. In retaliation, the Israeli government recruits a group of Mossad agents to track down and execute those responsible for the attack.

Review by SilentDawn 6

85

A world without reason - Spielberg, from the first 'revenge' murder, lingers on the faces of both sides. In each account, it doesn't get easier, but the motions become familiar, and Spielberg's dollies, zooms, one-ers etc. are jolting in a formulaic sense; moral muddiness in a constant loop, rising and climaxing, blurring the lines of routine and passion. Basically two hours of Spielberg showing off and yet he confines his skill to a narrative of accomplishment inherently tied to atrocity, with each off-the-cuff set-piece concluding with panache while the images spill with blood and fire, silence and annihilated bodies. That the film's distinct focus on sex - the characters' lack of it and the safety it can provide - is perverted by violence and trauma speaks to the collective pain which clouds up and rains down during the simplest, most primal of moments between people. When bloodshed is currency, there's no other way to pay.

Review by Will Sloan

First viewing since Boxing Day 2005, when I was 16. Probably the toughest Spielberg joint. This is the one you point to when you want to prove that he doesn’t just do treacle and happy endings, like how you might say “Hey, Paul McCartney can rock, check out Helter Skelter.” I like that after 2.5 hours of intrigue and killing, we end up in a world that is definitively a worse place than it was at the start. This separates Munich from other “grown up” Spielberg movies about historical atrocities like Amistad and Schindler’s List, which tend to focus on the heroic figures who helped stop the madness. Pretty funny this opened wide on Christmas, I mean it’s a downer!

Review by David Chen 3

“You could have a kitchen like this someday. It costs dearly, but home always does.”

This movie is nonstop. A thrilling collection of set pieces whose overall effect is to show the human cost of an endless cycle of bloodshed. 

The thing I took away the most on this viewing was the juxtaposition of middle class bourgeois existence with the machinery of death. Towards the beginning of the film, Geoffrey Rush’s character briefs Avner on who he is to kill and how he is to do it, all while walking along a peaceful seaside beach with children playing in front of them. In many of the characters’ minds, one leads to the other — the pleasantness we all take for granted exists because people dare to sacrifice for their country. 

The military and state apparatus insist that only through killing can your home finally be secured. The question this movie asks, quite hauntingly: What if it’s not?

Review by Adam Nayman 2

Doting (and vengeful) mothers; surrogate (and absent) fathers; the (im?)possibility  and (im)morality of doing your parents proud. I’d forgotten how much of Kushner’s script focuses on family as a source of motivation and also rationalization, over and over; “I won’t hesitate to kill other peoples’ children if you hurt my children,” Avner says, speaking not to one of the architects of the Munich massacre but a fellow Israeli, who’s standing in an office in front of a blue and white flag. There are some pat parallels here and some tidy ironies (the Al Green music cue is the cringiest kind of clever) but also plenty of mess: the hotel room blown apart so violently that the couple staying one suite over suffer grievous injuries along with the intended target; the dead woman’s robe falling away from her naked body, followed by the instruction to “leave it.”
