Destiny's Son

1962

斬る

Directed by Kenji Misumi

Synopsis

In Edo-period Japan, Shingo is born the son of an assassin who was executed for murdering her lord’s concubine. Decades later, his adoptive family is massacred for keeping the secret. Armed with an unbeatable swordfighting technique, Shingo embarks on the path of the masterless samurai in search of vengeance and a path beyond his own ignominious origins.

Review by Filipe Furtado

An education on death. 70 elliptical minutes, which reduces action to minimal essential gestures, all of it has something to do with killing. A life and what is getting passed along is a notion of survival in a feudal society that can only be expressed through violent power. One can decide if Destiny's Son is far too human or completely absent in the same. The certainty is that there's only one possible release available. Misumi is one of cinema's greatest formalists: the control of space, the combination of sets, framing, and movement have few peers. It includes perhaps the best swordsplay I've seen in one of those.

Review by nrh

Elliptical and unsettling film from Kenji Misumi, made the same year as his iconic Tale of Zatoichi.

Misumi covers several decades in his 70 minute running time; scenes are often a collection of short, disconnected images, repeated (in flashback) or rhymed. Little action, just enough to connect the plot in a repeating web of tragic (and futile) death.

Review by dirtylaundri

A 70 minute epic, spanning decades and generations, structured like an episodic adventure tale but shot through with an almost surreal sense of predestination. Review by Neill Shaughness 3

The protagonist uses a style of sword play so unconventional, that his opening stance alone gets him branded as an "heretical" swordsman. Samurai movies often need to craft an aspect of distinction for the main characters to make them stand out. A "heretical" swordsman is one of the coolest ways to do this that I've seen.
I don't have too much to say about this one. Some of the fight choreography looks a bit silly, but action from 1962 rarely holds up. Review by Jos 4.0 🍉

aka Destiny’s Child 

From the filmmaker behind the Zatoichi series and Lone Wolf films. Takes such pleasure in visual storytelling and camera flourishes. The sun is centred in closeup as a sword blade slices in front of it, a parent forced to kill their partner, symbolically slicing their son in half. The way the light hits a blade as water is poured over it is supernatural. I watch Misumi’s samurai films not for some pulpy urge to see steel carve into meat but for this kind of meditative, slow, tranquil journey. And then a little meat slicing is cool. Nihilistic, meditative, tranquil meat-slicing. It’s classier. 

(aka Kiru on Criterion)

It's not really the origin or circumstances of Shingo's early life that push Destiny's Son's plot forward (like it would in most genre films), it's plain old death. Death just keeps finding this poor guy. And solitude. And how will he respond this time? Elusive at first, but unbelievably poetic and visually stunning, Destiny's Son was a really pleasant surprise.
