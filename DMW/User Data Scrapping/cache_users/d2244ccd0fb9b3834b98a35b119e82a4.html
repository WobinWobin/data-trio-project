Mandabi

1968

Directed by Ousmane Sembène

Synopsis

A money order from a relative in Paris throws the life of a Senegalese family man out of order. He deals with corruption, greed, problematic family members, the locals and the changing from his traditional way of living to a more modern one.

Review by David Jenkins 2

Sembene’s tightest, most focused film. Just one of the most convincing, withering and entertaining films I’ve seen about the incompatibility of capitalism and the human ego. And with one of the most horrendously, laughably self-important lead characters ever -  which makes monitoring his downfall all the more delicious. And yet, despite the all-encompassing bile tsunami, the film, like all good comedies, segues into tragedy by its final reel, with humanity slumped on the floor, penniless, clueless and dazed. From what I’ve seen it might be Sembene’s best, most complete film (though would say the thrillingly wild Moolaadé is still my favourite).

Could there be anything worse than the bureaucracy that’s preventing him from cashing in the money order? Yes. The neighbors in his own town. How quickly someone can turn against another person when money gets involved!

Something as simple as cashing in a money order shouldn’t be problematic, but it is.

Review by Vincent Winther

Mandabi is all about what you can buy and what you can’t buy. How money changes what’s possible or at least what you perceive to be possible. 

Ousmane Sembène tells a story about a man whose incidental luck ends up sending him down a path of sorrow and deceit.

When Ibrahim (Makhouredia Gueye) tries to cash in his money order, he runs into people in need of money or people trying to get a cut of the sum. 

“That’s how things are these days, nobody can be trusted”

Ibrahim constantly runs into bureaucratic and human obstacles, keeping him from achieving his goal. Needing to rely on other people to get what he proves futile, however, without the help of others he gets nothing.

“To tell you the truth, decency has become a sin in this country. And that's why I'm going to become a wolf among wolves”

Review by Graham 2

"I'm the husband here! Only I can decide what to do!"

In his second feature, Ousmane Sembène created this vision of a poverty ridden 60s Senegal, where penniless Ibrahima receives an unexpected gift from a distant relative. In a land burnt by colonialism and with corruption at every turn, Ibrahima struggles to get his hands on the cash while those all around him have their hands out for a piece of his windfall.

Starkly real, often funny and packed with confronting cultural honesty, Mandabi is a great example of the Director's style and of Senegalese cinema done right.

Review by Sally Jane Black

This film is a dark but funny satire featuring a Senegalese man's attempts to cash in a money order that belabors its point just a bit too much and gets a little too on-the-nose in the end. It doesn't have Xala's better grasp on symbolism, instead focusing on more direct situations to comment on the corruption and woes of Senegalese life.

Honestly, there's heart here. Ibrahim spends a lot of time giving to others in need, trusting those he probably shouldn't, and striving to make things right (even if his tendency toward mistreating his wives pissed my mother off). If that had been the focus, I'd be much more pleased. Instead, the film gets darker and darker and ends in a manner that did not satisfy.

December count: 77/100.

Review by Alex 2

Watched on Criterion.

Following on from his debut Black Girl, Ousmane Sembène takes it back to his roots with his follow-up. Mandabi takes place entirely in Dakar, Senegal, and follows unemployed Muslim man Ibrahima as he tries to cash a generous money order sent to him from his nephew working in France. Ibrahima and his two wives are struggling to stay afloat, and with the difficulty he finds in obtaining the money, and constant handouts to friends and neighbours, their future looks far from secure.
