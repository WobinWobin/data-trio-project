Los Angeles Plays Itself

2003

Directed by Thom Andersen

Synopsis

From its distinctive neighborhoods to its architectural homes, Los Angeles has been the backdrop to countless movies. In this dazzling work, Andersen takes viewers on a whirlwind tour through the metropolis' real and cinematic history, investigating the myriad stories and legends that have come to define it, and meticulously, judiciously revealing the real city that lives beneath.

Review by Alice Stoehr 5

A mammoth essay that moves with wry humor and encyclopedic thoroughness in these widening loops through and around Hollywood's depictions of Los Angeles. It's a cornucopia, pulling clips from hundreds of movies (indie obscurities, trashy erotic thrillers, Chinatown) and organizing them into an overarching narrative. At first, it seems to be mostly about the film industry and Andersen's bitterness toward it; he rails against its continuity errors ("silly geography makes for silly movies"), its popularization of the term "L.A.," and its association of modern architecture with plutocratic villainy. Review by Matthew Christman 1

"Is there any other city where the police put their motto in quotation marks?"

Damn, that's a great line. It's too bad there aren't more of them. For a three-hour explication of Los Angeles on film, there's a relative lack of extended, textured arguments. For instance, the difference between "high tourist" and "low tourist" directors seems to come down to "likes LA, doesn't like LA," and since Andersen never really advances a heuristic for viewing the city, a lot of his critiques come off as gripes or frustrated civil boosterism. But that doesn't blunt the power of the images: the section on the extinct downtown neighborhood of Bunker Hill is downright revelatory.

Also, dude sounds just like Billy Bob Thornton.

Review by nathaxnne [goodbye <3] 10

The clip movie apotheosis of material historiographic montage. A sprawling visual essay truly befitting its topic. There is lots and lots to get into and get out of this movie, and critics far smarter and more qualified than myself have certainly done so and will continue to do so. What i dig about it, beyond its idiosyncratic and personal vantage point, are little things, like the love given to Messiah Of Evil, or the stunningly argued defense of Blade Runner as a near-utopian vision of a thriving multicultural, walkable metropolis free of traffic jams and corporate sterility.
