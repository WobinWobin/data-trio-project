Marilyn Monroe had that it factor. She was gorgeous, bubbly, hilarious, and vibrant on screen. But she could also act.

Monroe didn't do too many movies like Don't Bother to Knock. I'd honestly say this movie is one of a kind in her catalogue. 1953's Niagara is her other noir-ish picture, but it's still filtered through a "dumb blonde" persona. She's also fetishized in Niagara like it's a Hitchcock movie. She proved time and time again she was wonderful in comedy, but I wish she had gotten more roles like Nell in her career. Review by theriverjordan 19

America’s postwar trauma emerges from being stuffed away in the shadows of the national conscious in “Don’t Bother to Knock.“ 

An attempt by Marilyn Monroe to appeal to critical critique as to her acting ability, the film painfully intertwines her personal struggles with those of the country as a whole. 

Monroe assumes the role of a self-destructive small town transplant to New York, who takes on a night of nanny duties at the (still standing today!) McKinley Hotel. What unfolds in the plot’s real time narrative is something of a meta reflection on Monroe’s own life journey. 

Her mousey character of Nell Forbes gradually disguises herself in glamorous stolen clothes, which do little to hide her clearly evident mental suffering.…

As a film about mental illness made in the 1950s, Don't Bother to Knock is unexpectedly sensitive, not only with regard to its troubled central character (Marilyn Monroe as Nell), but also when it comes to the people around her. Within just 75 short minutes, director Roy Ward Baker and screenwriter Daniel Taradash manage to allow considerable growth in Richard Widmark's cynical pilot character, Jed, and treat characters that would sometimes be mocked or marginalized — middle-aged parents, a strange, awkward elevator man — with tremendous grace, shaping them into full human beings rather than the caricatures they might have been in the hands of others.

From British-born journeyman director Roy Ward Baker comes Don't Bother to Knock—a passable 1952 psychodrama which further substantiates my impression that people everywhere were generally unhinged in the immediate postwar era. I love movies that are set in hotels, so this spoke to me instantly. Not much happens, at least not externally. It's a small story, a sad one, too, capturing two people during a vulnerable night in which a handful of inner demons want to come out to play.
