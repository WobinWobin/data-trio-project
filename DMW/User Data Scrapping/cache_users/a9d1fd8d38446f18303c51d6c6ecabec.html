Shadows

1958

Directed by John Cassavetes

Synopsis

A film not to be missed.

The relationship between Lelia, a light-skinned black woman, and Tony, a white man is put in jeopardy when Tony meets Lelia’s darker-skinned jazz singer brother, Hugh, and discovers that her racial heritage is not what he thought it was.

Review by mia lee vicino 3

breaking in my cassavetes boxset with his directorial debut, SHADOWS, a probe into the complications of interracial relationships in late 50s new york. the erratic jazz score is a definite highlight, brilliantly mirroring the improvisational nature of the dialogue — makes sense that cassavetes and cast came up with the roots for the film in acting workshops, as some scenes do have the exploratory, push-pull dynamic of a drama school exercise. yeah, it’s a bit sloppy overall, but when you’re helping to pioneer the independent film movement, slack can be cut!

Way more impressionistic and less didactic than I'd remembered, with a jazzy rhythm that didn't really require Mingus to gild the lily. Unfortunately, whatever insightful or at least entertaining observations I might have made abandoned my addled head sometime during the past two weeks. Review by Jake Cole

Much funnier than I'd expected, especially in the scene where the racist white boyfriend comes back to Lelia/Ben/Hugh's apartment and tries to convey his apologies for running away as Ben stonefacedly assures the man he will relate the white boy's platitudes of "There's no difference to us" to his sister. Elsewhere, the great pleasure of this taboo-breaking feature is how unself-conscious it is about presenting confrontational subject matter, treating its characters as being normal (as they, of course, are) and sublimating its racial tension through subtly, deftly performed scenes like the aforementioned one. Displays its makers' filmmaking inexperience at every turn, but also how they used that to their advantage.

Review by Edgar Cochran ✝️🍋

You can cheer for Ben Hur and Some Like it Hot as much as you want, but truth is Cassavetes' "improvisatory" examination of daily life is several times much more important, socially speaking. A set of common characters with multiple personalities arise undeniable psychological clashes. It's perhaps also important to note that this directorial debut hit American cinemas before Godard's work traveled abroad, so you may add the "improvisation" excuse right there, in Cassavetes' style. Nonetheless, it is a wonderful contribution to the independent cinema branch.

Review by nick

Shadows is merely baby steps for a more accomplished Cassavetes in the future, but the potential is already there.

It takes guts to make a black indie story in the 50s, and on top of racism, Shadows encompasses many other themes in its highly spontaneous manner, which can be a boon and a curse.

Shadows centers around a mixed-raced, independent woman who takes the wheel of her romantic life into her own hands, although the casting of an Italian American actress as the white-passing protagonist can be questionable. It's almost entirely dialogue-driven and episodic, and its unapologetic rawness renders the whole story all over the place, although Cassavetes obviously attempted to salvage this project with abundant style. Overall not my favorite Cassavetes.
