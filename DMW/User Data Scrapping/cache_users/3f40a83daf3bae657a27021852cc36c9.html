Synopsis

It’s not stealing if you don’t get caught.

In a sedate Massachusetts suburb circa 1970, unemployed family man and amateur art thief J.B. Mooney sets out on his first heist. With the museum cased and accomplices recruited, he has an airtight plan. Or so he thinks.

Review by Thomas Flight 46

Understated might be an understatement. To fully appreciate much of what Reichardt usually (but especially here) you have to understand how her deconstruction of narratives relates to her deconstruction of masculine tropes.

A deadbeat dad tries to escape his feeling that he was "meant for greater things" by casting himself as a real life character in the plot of a genre film, all Reichardt does is have show us (with a sort of wry smile) how that would probably actually play out. To defy the plot convention is to defy the masculine hubris.

What I appreciate about Reichardt's cuts to the bone of her characters is that it isn't done in a mean-spirited way but instead with both tenderness and firmness.

Also it's just funny and a good time.

Full review in my coverage of Cannes Week 2.

Review by claira curtis 13

This review may contain spoilers. I can handle the truth.

What I love about The Mastermind’s bumbling protagonist is that he’s not bad at stealing. Our introduction to James shows a man who’s aware of his surroundings, skillfully cautious, and capable of pulling off the heist without even a hint of failure. It’s in the moments after this first theft that we see his ultimate shortcomings. He can’t connect. He has no patience for his energetic son. His family— for the most part— doesn’t slow down when he falls behind to tie a shoe. He’s there only physically, mind perpetually elsewhere, and it shows in how disengaged he is. The lack of connection he has with others is his ultimate downfall. Without genuine connection with those around him, he misreads…

Cut to 2025 - the first movie we saw in theaters as a married couple just so happened to be Kelly Reichardt's The Mastermind. That's a cosmic aberration if there ever was one.

How's the film? Cozy, gentle, heavy. It takes on the tone of waking up on a bitter autumn day and taking the dog out for a morning walk only to realize you probably should've worn a jacket.

Review by shookone 2

first 30 minutes are a free jazzy endeavor, keeping you on your toes what to expect next. once Reichardt gives us the 5-plus-feeling-like-15-minutes sequence of O'Connor hiding the paintings in the pig stall you'll realize she might be mocking us here. maybe she thinks she's doing an Bresson impersonation of an Oceans heist type film, that's a wild guess though.

Alana Haim is completely wasted, Davis and Camp are fun and the only thing giving you something remotely to chew on for an interpretation, being the reactionary parents pushing their son to his questionable life choices. still, The Mastermind feels mostly like an odd concept fizzling out by the end of a story of a loser's rabbit hole whose journey you'll never be able to grasp.
