Howl from Beyond the Fog

2019

狭霧の國

Directed by Daisuke Sato

Synopsis

Set during the Meiji reformation era in a small village in Kyushu, Japan. The story revolves around a young boy named Izana and a blind woman named Takiri, the two encounter the large monster Nebula who since ancient times was feared as the god of lake Amenosagiri. Theme of the film focuses on the Japanese concept of light and darkness, as told by puppetry and model miniaturization of the films’ world with practical special effects by Keizo Murase.

Review by Rafael "Mister Movie" Jovine 12

68th Review for The Collab Weekly Movie Watch

Another wonderful example of someone who took "I'm going to play with my toys and make something epic out of it" rather too literally.

If I'm being honest, a lot of my high rating come from technical work. Though I appreciate the existential tone of the plot, I found myself more impressed by the film's technical and aesthetic accomplishments than its narrative. The cinematography is excellent; there are moments when you forget whether you're seeing a live-action or 3D animated film; the colors are vibrant; and the scale is astounding.

All in all, a fantastic film that proves that the world still has creative individuals who, if not pushing the envelope, at least attempt to produce something original.

Review by Justin Decloux 4

A beautiful 34-minute ALL PUPPET Kaiju short about a blind woman befriending a sightless loch ness monster. Of course, greedy land developers get involved and it all goes to hell.

I usually scoff when someone goes "I want to make a giant monster movie where they don't punch each other!" because that usually means it'll be a bore, but the singular artistic vision on display in HOWL totally works. And the short running time means there's no room for flab, just gorgeous shots and enough emotional undercurrent to tug on any monster lover's heartstrings.

Review by Ziglet_mir

Viewed with THE COLLAB

Startling dins echo from the barreled throat of the beast, akin to the eeriness of the distant and unsettling train sounds in Lynch's Rabbits, where the uncanny valley reigns supreme. Or maybe just the animalistic base of Siren Head. Altogether the creature sections are the most evocative to no greater purpose, with a secondary narrative (or actually the main one) that examines the inner machinations of what Mother Earth should be used for. Probably another Eastern critique on the human impact toward the environment. Would have been cooler if the Kaiju represented the animosity between two star-crossed lovers and their families. This starts to sound a lot like Colossal, which may not be an upgrade. The former would be cool with puppets and siren-sounding monstrosities from the deep though. Review by James Atkinson 2

Land developers come between a blind girl and a Kaiju.

A very artistic short movie using all puppets. The creativity that has gone into this, mostly made by one man movie is pretty astounding. A lot of time was spent getting the sense of scale right among the Kaiju, humans and sets. I wish the human puppets could have had some expression but this was done on a very limited budget. The foggy scenes were the stand out parts as it gave the shots atmosphere and hid some of the budgetary constraints.

Academy goes to Daisuke Sato for his time and effort put into making this very unique film.
