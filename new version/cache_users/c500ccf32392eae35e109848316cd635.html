Ratcatcher

1999

Directed by Lynne Ramsay

Synopsis

James Gillespie is 12 years old. The world he knew is changing. Haunted by a secret, he has become a stranger in his own family. He is drawn to the canal where he creates a world of his own. He finds an awkward tenderness with Margaret Anne, a vulnerable 14 year old expressing a need for love in all the wrong ways, and befriends Kenny, who possesses an unusual innocence in spite of the harsh surroundings.

Review by Criterion 9

In her breathtaking and assured debut feature, Lynne Ramsay creates a haunting evocation of a troubled Glasgow childhood. Set during Scotland’s national garbage strike of the mid-1970s, Ratcatcher explores the experiences of a poor adolescent boy as he struggles to reconcile his dreams and his guilt with the abjection that surrounds him. Utilizing beautiful, elusive imagery, candid performances, and unexpected humor, Ramsay deftly contrasts urban decay with a rich interior landscape of hope and perseverance, resulting in a work at once raw and deeply poetic.

This edition arrives on October 19, 2021. To learn more or pre-order, visit Criterion.com.

Review by r. marsh 4

Lynne Ramsay understands children. By setting her marvelous debut in Glasgow during the national garbage strike of the mid-1970's, the writer-director ensures that not one of the city's wayward youth has it easy. Many grow up too fast, become sexual deviants, and treat those who refuse to mature at their inappropriately rapid pace as playthings. Such is the case with James, the endearing protagonist, who in spite of all the grime and cruelty of the times is able to look ahead to the future and see a glimmer of hope. There's a scene somewhere in the middle, when little James hops aboard the nearest bus heading straight to the middle of nowhere, that highlights the young man's desire to escape…

Review by Dirk Diggler 6

A truly special little film. It's poetic realism at its best, as it depicts a young boy's simple coming-of-age story in the grim and stinky, garbage and rat infested streets of Glasgow in the 70's with such a poetic, meditative touch. The imagery is breathtaking, I loved the music choices, and every performance feels so natural and real. It's bleak, gritty and melancholic yet somewhat hopeful, and there's so many beautiful shots and moments throughout, but the incredible, foreboding opening shot, and the first little, almost otherworldly scene of James in the field takes the cake for me. Can't believe this was a debut, this is wonderful filmmaking from start to finish. I'm floored by it. THAT SMILE

Review by allain♡ 11

I lost count on how many rats I saw but I assure you that at least one was caught. Ratcatcher portrays the life of a twelve-year old kid in the 1970s in an hour and a half film. The movie evoked multiple statements in familial relationships, friendships, poverty, dreams, and death in a slow yet chaotic fashion. The distinct realism of the film made it more impactful to me, showing the harsh reality of the past and how lucky I am to not experience any of it. Eadie’s life will make you question a lot of things, for better and for worse. The subtle yet effective way of plot and character building and the astonishing cinematography will make you think of this film for days to come.

Review by Sethsreviews 3

Gloomy visual poetry capturing the spirit of the working-class way of life while displaying turmoil through the eyes of an isolated child and the mental processing of one minor act that led to catastrophe. Bleak and often despairing, but loaded with charming image after charming image. This is what I would consider to be pretty much the gold standard of British social realism. I got to see the 4k restoration of this for the 25th anniversary at my local cinema, and it looked absolutely brilliant on the big screen. Lynne Ramsay is brilliant!
