King of New York

1990

Directed by Abel Ferrara

Synopsis

Not everyone who runs a city is elected.

A former drug lord returns from prison determined to wipe out all his competition and distribute the profits of his operations to New York's poor and lower classes in this stylish and ultra violent modern twist on Robin Hood.

Review by Neil Bahadur 21

"I thought people like me were the legal system."

One of the seminal films of the 1990's...almost perfect as a stylistic exercise yet concurrently filled with such political commitment that it's simply overwhelming. Everything Ferrara hates is in this movie, including it's form which he criticizes in the commentary. "This is so uptight I can't even breathe. I'm glad I made this film because I would never make a film like this again. This is fascistic filmmaking." He's partially right, the film is incredibly tight, so tight that nearly every shot, every cut, every object has a specific purpose and function. It's some of the most "perfect" mis-en-scene I've ever witnessed, even down to the sheen on the wineglasses near…

Review by matt lynch 3

Never sacrifices thrills or atmosphere or style, never skips one opportunity for digression, never misses any chance to shock, doesn't sell short a single member of its incredibly stacked cast of future stars -- not to mention Walken's absolutely towering performance -- and still has time for a stinging critique of white capital's exploitation of black labor. It's weird that I don't watch this like four times a year.

Review by Josh Lewis 5

A cops vs. gangsters movie with a deceptively complicated moral lens about systems of ostensible righteousness connected to systems of violent force. The contradiction of philanthropy built on exploitation; the redistribution of blood money. Probably Walken's finest performance (particularly his sudden transitions from charm to dead-eyed wielding of power) and Ferrara's fullest stylistic expression of his brute nihilism. "Build a brand new day, dream on."

Full discussion on ep 185 of my podcast SLEAZOIDS.

Review by Jake Cole 2

Ferrara’s most classically formal masterpiece does just as much to fit into the mainstream as it does to illustrate just how boldly the director diverges from all but the most daring of his ostensible peers. The politics of the film, of a white kingpin bossing around mostly Black and Latino underlings while enjoying entry into the rarefied, extremely white upper crust of society based on a shared exploitation of the lower rungs, are clear without ever being clumsily insisted upon. Numerous scenes show the ways that both Frank’s underlings and even just Black people in the orbit of service work in rich areas always occupy a lower status without Ferrara ever resorting to so much as a pointed insert shot…
