Leaving Las Vegas

1995

Directed by Mike Figgis

Synopsis

I love you... The way you are.

Ben Sanderson, an alcoholic Hollywood screenwriter who lost everything because of his drinking, arrives in Las Vegas to drink himself to death. There, he meets and forms an uneasy friendship and non-interference pact with prostitute Sera.

Review by DirkH 24

This film kicks my teeth in in the most beautiful way every time I watch it.

Whenever an artist manages to combine and contrast beauty and reality I'm always sucked in without reservation, often leaving me wrecked but smiling. Figgis, Cage and Shue made promises here they unfortunately never kept in their later careers.

The notion of a man leaving everything behind to drink himself to death in the capital of sin is as strong as it is fraught with the danger of skirting melodrama. Figgis manages to walk this tightrope really well as he is as unrelenting for his audience as Cage's character is for himself. He cuts no corners and unveils two lives that meet, linked in misfortune,…

Review by Rocky Ibarra

It’s never occurred to me that Leaving Las Vegas is a film that revolves around depression, alcoholism or prostitution; you’ll have to include scenes where the hardships of coming from those backgrounds are the matter but the film struck me for a couple of reasons that are not associated with any of these things. Mainly, it is a touching story with realistic people in it in real-life relationships. It is about people with those backgrounds but the backgrounds don’t really say anything about their personalities. They can be tender. Much of the film is about the capacity to contain hope in the midst of knowing that we are empty and hollow, and that there will always be people who care…

STARRING: NIC FUUUUUUUUUUUUUCKIIIIIIING!!!!! CAGE (THE COPPOLA SAGA)

Cage drinks to death his way into that Oscar gold in this drama about two broken souls finding solace in each other as they navigate their lives in Las Vegas. While one yearns to depart from this world, the other discovers the means to endure another day.

Cage in a rare downbeat role where the unhinged is replaced by the depressive. He portrays a sad, tragic character whose self-destruction has escalated to unprecedented levels. Like, you know you’ve reached a low point when the bartender is like, “Bro, cut it out.” Shue is fantastic, also in a very quiet but powerful performance as this sex worker with a heart, but don’t go expecting…

Review by Will Sloan

Whenever Cage talks about acting in interviews, he talks about his desire to move away from naturalism, or to find a way to combine naturalism with the "expressionism" of silent-film acting. I get excited hearing him talk about these ideas, and then wonder if he uses his dozens of under-the-radar, direct-to-VOD jobs as opportunities to experiment. But then I check him out in something like Rage or Left Behind and my excitement immediately deflates.

Anyway, Cage absolutely pulls it off here. Elisabeth Shue also very good.

Review by Ben Peterson 2

"You can never, ever, ask me to stop drinking. Do you understand?" 
——————
"I do. I really do."

This line and moment ring more true than any line or moment in any film about alcohol abuse in the history of cinema. This is the most depressing movie and it isn’t. The levity this excellent script brings to the table to save it from being oppressively dismal could only be pulled off by the actor Nicolas Cage. A depressive movie that even begs for rewatches, and rewards them, because of performance and the direction of them by Mike Figgis. It's anything but an ordinary and familiar film. I think we forget how much of an edge Leaving Las Vegas has - especially in the last few minutes when it forays very much into European Art House territory.

"It's okay. It's okay."
